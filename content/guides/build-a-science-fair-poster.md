A poster is a communication interface, not the project itself. Its job is to make your reasoning and evidence easy to inspect while you explain the work.

Society for Science’s judging criteria emphasize the student’s understanding, independence, evidence, limitations, and interview—not expensive printing or decoration. Strong design cannot rescue weak research; it can stop strong research from being hidden.

## Give the poster one reading path

From several feet away, a viewer should be able to answer:

1. What did you ask or build?
2. What did you do?
3. What happened?
4. Why does it matter?

Use a question-led title, a short statement of significance, and one dominant result. Arrange the remaining sections around that path.

::

Include what your fair requires. A practical anatomy is:

- title, student name, school, and collaborators when appropriate;
- research question or engineering problem;
- only the background needed to understand it;
- hypothesis or design goal when relevant;
- method or engineering process;
- system/experimental diagram;
- results and statistical analysis;
- limitations and sources of uncertainty;
- conclusion, impact, and next step;
- concise references, acknowledgments, and approval identifiers as required.

## Make the method visible

A methods diagram should show the evidence path, not decorate it. Include sample/case selection, conditions, measurement, and comparison. Put critical values—sample size, timing, concentration, model version, number of trials—in labels or a short caption.

## Let results occupy the space

Results should be the largest visual area after the title. Prefer plots, annotated images, concise tables, and diagrams to paragraphs that merely restate them.

Every main figure needs:

- labeled axes and units;
- visible sample size or a caption that states it;
- defined error bars;
- a caption that states the takeaway;
- accessible color and another cue besides color alone;
- enough resolution and contrast to read at viewing distance.

::graph-comparison-diagram
::

## Separate result from interpretation

Place the measured outcome near the graph. Put the bounded interpretation beside or below it. This makes it harder to accidentally turn a single-dataset result into a universal claim.

::

## Edit at actual size

Print one letter-size reduction to test hierarchy, then inspect the full layout at intended viewing distance. If the main question, result, and conclusion disappear when reduced, the hierarchy is too flat. If a paragraph cannot be cut, ask whether it belongs in your spoken explanation instead.

Avoid walls of text, tiny references, decorative 3D charts, low-contrast gradients, rainbow category colors, and clip art that does not carry evidence.

## Practice with the poster

Do not recite from top left to bottom right. Face the listener, open with the problem and central result, then point to evidence when it answers a question. Be ready to explain what you did personally, what failed, what your mentor contributed, and what remains uncertain.

## See real work responsibly

The [Society for Science finalist abstract database](https://abstracts.societyforscience.org/) shows how real projects phrase purposes and findings. Abstracts are not complete posters, and inclusion is not permission to copy wording or artwork. Use official university poster guidance and examples as references; create your own figures and visual system.
