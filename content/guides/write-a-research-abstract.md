An abstract lets a reader decide what was done, what was found, and whether the full project is relevant. It is not an introduction with the results removed.

## Use five moves

1. **Problem and context:** one or two sentences establishing the specific need.
2. **Question or design goal:** the exact objective.
3. **Method:** the study design, sample/data, comparison, and primary metric.
4. **Major result:** the most decision-relevant quantitative outcome, with uncertainty when appropriate.
5. **Conclusion:** what the result supports, its boundary, and the implication.

Draft the result and conclusion first. They determine which background and method details deserve scarce words.

Before drafting, create an evidence inventory:

| Item           | What must be known                                                     |
| -------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Objective      | The exact question or design goal                                      |
| Study          | Experiment, observation, design test, simulation, proof, or other form |
| Evidence       | Sample/unit, conditions, and data source                               |
| Comparison     | Control, baseline, reference, or alternative                           |
| Primary result | Estimate with units and uncertainty when supported                     |
| Boundary       | The strongest limitation on the conclusion                             |
| Ownership      | What the student actually did                                          |
| Destination    | Word limit, required fields, authorship, and formatting rules          |

If final results do not exist, do not write predicted results as if observed. A proposal abstract must be labeled and structured according to the destination’s proposal rules.

## Compare two abstracts

**Weak**

> Plastic pollution is a huge problem around the world. In this project, different biodegradable materials were investigated using many tests. The results were significant and showed that our new material performed much better. This material could revolutionize packaging and save the environment.

The question, comparison, test conditions, numbers, and limitations are absent. “Significant” and “much better” carry no inspectable meaning.

**Improved**

> Low-cost compostable packaging must retain strength during use while degrading under disposal conditions. We tested whether a starch–fiber composite could maintain tensile strength for seven days and lose mass during controlled composting. Three formulations and a commercial paper baseline were tested across 12 independently prepared specimens per condition. The best formulation retained 82% of initial tensile strength after seven days and lost 31% mass after 28 days, compared with 94% strength retention and 8% mass loss for the baseline. Under these laboratory conditions, the composite improved degradation at the cost of lower short-term strength; moisture sensitivity, toxicity, and manufacturing scale remain untested.

The improved version gives the reader something to evaluate. The numbers are illustrative; your abstract must use your own verified results.

### Edit from notes, not from adjectives

Lab-note version: “We did a lot of tests on several materials and noticed that sample B seemed to break down faster, although it was also not as strong.”

Evidence-led revision: “Across 12 independently prepared specimens per condition, formulation B lost 31% mass after 28 days but retained 82% of initial tensile strength after seven days; the paper baseline lost 8% mass and retained 94% strength.”

The revision identifies unit, comparison, outcomes, time, and tradeoff. It does not add importance the measurements did not establish.

Now reverse-outline the abstract. Label each sentence **context**, **objective**, **method**, **result**, or **conclusion**. Cut repeated jobs. Verify that the conclusion introduces no new result, mechanism, population, or application. Check that the title does not promise a broader scope than the abstract delivers.

Engineering abstracts may emphasize criteria, constraints, prototype iterations, and final performance. Computational or mathematical abstracts may emphasize assumptions, method, bound, counterexample, or validated behavior. Do not force every project into biomedical experimental language.

## Keep ownership and rules visible

Describe only the work you performed. If a mentor’s laboratory supplied data, equipment, code, or procedures, follow the destination’s acknowledgment and authorship rules. Do not blur the mentor’s work into the student procedure.

Every fair, journal, conference, or workshop has its own length, format, content, and authorship rules. The [Society for Science abstract guidance](https://www.societyforscience.org/isef/how-to-write-an-isef-abstract/) is specific to ISEF, including its **250-word limit**; it is not a universal submission rule. Use the actual destination instructions linked from the opportunity.

## Cut without hollowing it out

Remove broad opening claims, literature lists, procedural trivia, undefined adjectives, repeated conclusions, and future-work wish lists. Keep the comparison, sample, primary metric, major number, and boundary of the claim.

Do a final word-budget pass in this order: remove throat-clearing; combine repeated context; replace vague phrases with one number; cut method trivia that does not affect interpretation; shorten the conclusion without widening it. Never delete the sample, comparison, or limitation merely to preserve an impressive opening sentence.

::guide-callout{title="Abstract audit" tone="action"}
Circle the question, method, baseline, sample, strongest number, and limitation. If one is missing, add it before polishing adjectives.
::
